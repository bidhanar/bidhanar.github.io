The Prisoner

Download PDF Back to Stories

It was a special day today. It had been three months since Garry was convicted and sentenced to life imprisonment for a crime he hadn’t committed. The time he had endured had weighed him down a lot. Today, he was just sitting on his bed, with his diary in his hand, reading his journal. His lawyer has instructed Garry to keep a journal, because prison life quickly changes people and one must know what they were before becoming one of the inmates.

He remembered the first day he came to this prison. His warden had met him at the gate and said, “Your freedom starts now!” — with a smirk on his face, which Garry now understood was not mockery, but pity. The first time he walked through the prison gates, he was devastated. His entire life was about to change. Everything he had been, everything he ever desired to be, was to be crushed by the fragility of the society that existed within these prison bars.

That first day, he was given a room with a bed — a small relief, at least he could live as an honored human being. His clothes were taken away and replaced with attire he wasn’t comfortable wearing, though he didn’t yet know what they signified. The first day in prison is always the hardest. He knew he didn’t belong there; it wasn’t his crime after all. He was paying for someone else’s.

He remembered the first night — sneaking into bed early and hugging his pillow tightly, desperate for any sense of human touch. He thought about the world he had left behind and cried until no tears were left. Garry had never felt so lonely — away from all the people he knew, his friends and his family. Of course, he could talk to them once a week, but that wasn’t enough for a man damned in this hellhole for eternity.

The next morning, when he woke up, the prisoner in the neighboring cell smiled at him and wished him good morning, trying to make him feel at home, even though this place could never be one. Every new prisoner was allotted their job on the first day, which brought Garry some solace — at least he had something to distract himself from this grim reality.

A person away from their family becomes alone, but a person away from themselves becomes lonely. Garry had never truly felt loneliness before — he’d always had friends to support him — but this was a new life. A life where he needed to act strong, so he wouldn’t get picked on by bullies.

He realized in the first week that prison wasn’t equal. The bullies here wielded more power than the rest, getting almost whatever they desired, while others were stuck in their sentences.

Lucky for him, Garry got the job as a cook. He had been one in his earlier life for 20 years, and that was all he knew. Whenever he was in the kitchen, he’d deliberately make himself forget the torments of prison life and focus only on making the best food he could. It was his little escape from the world around him.

As the weeks passed, the inmates became hooked on Garry’s cooking. They loved it. Unexpectedly, he earned a reputation among them. They would often look at him and wave — not because of who he was, but because of what he did — and even that was enough to give him a small ray of hope within these walls.

Gradually, he became more comfortable with the reality around him, accepting life as it was now, forgetting everything he once knew. Garry had always been an intuitive person, someone who liked studying people and their reactions. But he never imagined that he would one day become the subject of his own study.

He had thought, in the beginning, that people in prison would be eternally sad — grieving the loss of freedom. But to his surprise, the inmates laughed more than those who claimed to be free. Garry had read about the five stages of grief before his imprisonment. Maybe these people had already reached acceptance — he hadn’t yet. Every night, he still curled up in his bed, dreaming of his old life, his friends, and the little moments they’d shared.

Life isn’t to be lived thinking about how it should be, but by accepting how it is.

About two weeks into the prison, Garry found a friend— not just someone to talk to, but someone who understood him. Someone who had been through something similar and could show him a new way of seeing things. They met in the kitchen — his friend was a new recruit, recently assigned as a fellow cook.

The first time Garry saw this person, he somehow knew they’d become friends. They both carried that same insatiable, searching look in their eyes. One day, his friend came up to him and asked why he always seemed so sad. Garry replied that he felt purposeless in this place — that it was impossible to see anything positive here. “Everyone’s bound by rules,” he said. “Everyone acts out of fear. Not waking up on time is scary. Not following the warden’s orders is scary. How can anyone stay happy — or even alive — in a place governed by fear?”

In all his moments of sadness, Garry remembered his old life — where he wasn’t truly happy either, but at least he could make others happy and take solace in that.

A month later, he was invited to a prison event. It was one of the many programs in the prison meant to distract inmates from the grim reality of confinement. Garry had no interest in such events — he saw them as toys given to a toddler to distract him from a scraped knee. But his friend urged him to go. He tried making other friends, but more often than not, inmates here were illusioned and Garry quickly lost interest in talking to them.

His friend was unlike anyone he had ever met— calm, composed, always smiling, treating prison not as a curse but as a strange kind of blessing. Garry thought the inmate was delusional, yet he couldn’t deny how peaceful his friend seemed, just accepting the punishment blissfully, not even hating the people who were responsible for this. This perspective always moved Garry, he felt something inside, but couldn’t decide if it was happiness or envy. So, whenever he got a chance to better know his friend, he took it, and he decided to attend the event.

It was a play reenacting a past prison break. The story was about an inmate who realized that a life sentence here wasn’t worth living, and so he planned his escape. But fearing the consequences his escape might bring upon others, he hesitated — until one day, he could bear it no longer and asked his friends to join him, which they didn’t, appearing content with this imprisonment, so he fled alone. His actions led to tighter security and harsher conditions for everyone else.

The play portrayed the escapee as a villain. But Garry didn’t see it that way. He thought: caring for yourself becomes a sin when others don’t benefit from it. What’s the real difference between smartness and villainy, he wondered? Both lead a person to get their way at the cost of others. Maybe the difference lies in the effort to warn others — and this man had done just that. So, even though everyone saw him as the villain, Garry saw him as the hero. He thought about what the response of his friend would be if he had asked the same question, if he asked to just break free?

Prison life was strict. Every day began the same way — wake up, go to work, repeat. The better he worked, the more respect he earned from other inmates. After two months, he had grown used to his new life and new people. Still, Garry cried himself to sleep each night, missing his friends and his old freedom.

His fellow inmate — the one he had befriended — had long accepted that this life would be spent within these walls. They had gone beyond desire, realizing that wanting too much in prison could drive a man to do terrible things. Garry was deeply moved by his friend’s thoughts. Over the weeks, they talked every day — about life, meaning, pain, and the human mind.

Just last week as they were sitting on a bench, his friend said, “You know, I always felt like people don’t understand this place, a few people just remain delusional, and pretend to be happy, the others, the ones incapable of deluding themselves, must make a choice every day, whether this life is worth fighting for, or do they just want to escape. I always try to choose the former, I never could figure out what you would choose though.”

Garry never asked this question directly to himself, he had been deluding himself too, by daydreaming of escaping, when he knew he couldn’t make himself to do it. He smiled and asked for some time to answer this question.

Yesterday, while talking to his friend, Garry felt a warmth crawl through his chest — something he had never felt before. He never imagined he could feel something so beautiful, especially in a place like this. He could finally see why people still want to stay locked in, in this prison. It was because escaping would mean losing on the opportunities to experience the small moments of warmth, like he was experiencing now.

Prison life can be scary and sad, but if one finds a companion to share that pain with, it can be as bearable — even as meaningful — as the earlier. Garry no longer desired the freedom he once knew. He decided to choose this life, this life of pain, because she made it worth fighting for.

He put down his diary and got up from his bed, rushing towards the door. He took his keys and locked his apartment. He sprinted into the streets and took a cab, thinking about Samantha — and how being with her made him want this prison, and not escape it. He looked outside the window, big buildings all around him, his fellow inmates, working as hard as they could, in hope of getting something this world might not even have to offer. He faded into his thoughts, remembering the past 20 years as a prisoner in a prison, but now he was out in this world, a prison where the only difference is that most of the inmates do not realise that they are trapped. His stream of thoughts was broken by a text, from Samantha saying, “Got your text, I’ll see you there in an hour”. A smile came onto his lips, he had found his answer. He finally decided he was free.
