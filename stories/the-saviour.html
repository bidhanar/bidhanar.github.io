The Saviour

Download PDF Back to Stories

Hi Susan,

You know I don’t write much these days. I have gotten bored now, of repeating myself. I have talked to you a lot, about everything that I think, about every incomplete thought, of the wonders my mind is capable of imagining into existence. This year, I had finally gotten out of my rut, with your help of course. When I decided that I should go out and meet people, I was pretty terrified; I knew them so little, the only interactions I had with people were either professional or, if personal, were decades old. The world has changed, Susan. But today was very different, and I thought it required a message to you.

I met Sam today; he is a 16-year-old boy, just in his senior year at school, and he was a peculiar person. I was just picking up groceries when I saw Sam picking up his toiletries; he seemed sad, and with months of practice, I decided I had to help him. I had to save him from himself; I needed to. So I went up to him and asked, “Hi! You seem sad; is there any way I can help?” He was frozen for a minute; I mean, of course, seeing a guy twice your age come up to you and ask this warranted further investigation in his mind, but he finally decided to speak: “Hi! I don’t think you can help me; I just have a lot in my mind, regarding life and all. I am sure you don’t want to hear my story, but hey, thanks for asking!” Susan, you know I see pain; I honestly feel it, to the extent that I am now unsure whether I feel other things, or just the absence of pain sometimes. I had to help him, for you, Susan. I took him to the food court, ordered for both of us, and asked him, “Tell me, Sam, what’s on your mind? I would be happy to hear it; I believe the more you talk about something, the lighter it weighs on you. The only thing that people protect—their secrets—are important to them because they don’t tell people; if they ever do, that secret is lost and gets out of the mind. So, Sam, tell me your secret, if you want to.” Susan, the things Sam told me were beautiful; they were a true moment of emotions, a moment that etched into me. He said, “My father is ill. He was diagnosed with Alzheimer’s 6 years back, and now it’s nearing its end, getting worse. I don’t know what to do, how to deal with it. My mother is a housewife; she was married at a young age. I mean, who does that? Why did they need to? I am still angry at my grandparents for marrying her so young; she didn’t deserve that. She wasn’t allowed a formal education, and now she has taken up a few menial jobs, just so we can afford to live. I don’t like this. I am just very angry, but I am tired too; I don’t even know who I am angry with. So yeah, sometimes I can’t control the thoughts, and they spill on my face. Today was that. I am sorry for telling you all this; you are just a random stranger; you shouldn’t have to bear my burdens.” I was shocked, Susan, not at his condition—that didn’t interest me a mere bit—but the resilience of this boy. I asked him then, “Do you think if you found out who you’re angry at, things will feel better?” He said, “I guess; maybe I will have a direction; an enemy you know is better than a friend you don’t. But I have thought about it a lot, and I don’t think there is an answer to that question.” “I study the minds of people, Sam; let me ask you a few questions, and tell me your answers to them,” I said, and he agreed. I had a few generic things to ask first: “Do you love your father?” “I don’t know anymore. He is a great dad; I have always remembered him as a loving person, a caring person; he enrolled me in a good school, and I owe him everything, but—” he stopped; he hesitated to say the next part. In the time of this silence, I had imagined the worst: the abuse, the disconnect, everything. “But what, Sam?” I asked. “But he knew grandfather had Alzheimer’s, and there was a high probability of him getting it too. Why did he birth me into this world, with such a curse?” He had a point: when one knows that there is even a slight possibility of pain, without much of a positive, why would they bring a child into this world? Sam had a bad fate written even before he came into this world. But I needed to see if I could change his mind, so I said, “Sam, he also didn’t ask to be born, right? What you are feeling, I am pretty sure he must have felt too. If you give a hand to a drowning person, they would pull you in too; is that their fault, or just a survival instinct?” He took a pause, as if to think, but now I feel he already had his answer ready, because when he started, he said all of this in a repeated and rehearsed way: “Yeah, but either I think my father is weak or I think he is immoral. If he couldn’t stay alone, he should have taken it as his responsibility and stopped this punishment for me. Every day I live is more difficult than the other, and soon I will have to face everything I am seeing with him. I don’t know how to be happy anymore.” I understood his age; people are rebellious and angry all the time. I have experienced this myself, and I have seen yours too. The best way to deal with naive people is to start agreeing with them, and they will soon jump to protect their small, unformed ego. The food arrived: a tray of burgers and fries. I stopped this discussion and asked him to focus on the food. We started talking about other things: his hobbies, which were once playing basketball, were now just checking ads in the newspaper for part-time jobs with no age restriction. He had to leave school and stay at home because he had no money for tuition. Yeah, Susan, he lied earlier. He did say not all parts were bad; he got closer to his mother, his only companion in this pain. He couldn’t decide whose pain was more, but at least there was something they had in common, something to bond over. In the evenings, his mother used to make tea just for the two of them; they sat in their little courtyard, facing a railway line. On evenings like these, he felt a glimmer of hope.

Our food was over now, and we started walking; his home was close to mine, so we decided to walk to his home. Susan, it was a beautiful evening today, I tell you: sun deep in the sky, painting everything orange. The roads looked fiery, extending long, filled with vehicles, people in a rush, behind their own little lives, no one caring about Sam. As we crossed the park, with children playing on the swings, happy, deluded, while their parents sat beside, talking adult stuff, happy to see their children—whose lives they had destroyed—I said, “Sam, why do you think these people are happy?” He said, “Because they can provide for their children, I guess. But I am not sure if they should be.” I pretended to agree with him: “I think you are right, but aren’t we all social beings? You know, loneliness can kill people, and maybe worse, they may kill someone. I think they just choose to do the lesser damage and hold their problems within only a few people, who they call their family.” I turned to him and saw a sense of happiness and peace on his face, as if he had finally found someone who understood him. I too felt like he was a friend. He said, “Maybe you are right. But doesn’t that make a person just weak then? If they understand the gravity of the situation and still choose to make a family to loathe their pain on?” I still had to agree with him to make him arrive at my point naturally. “I guess they are weak, but why does it matter? To them, it’s not that big a discussion. What we deem as good or bad is just what the majority of people accept. Society accepts birthing as a natural process of life and welcomes it with celebrations and respect. Your social credit goes up; you end up less lonely because after a point, you won’t have much to share with people around you if you don’t live lives like theirs.” He let out a faint smile, as if everything he was feeling was coming from a place of despair and there was something in my words that felt like hope. He got curious and asked, “But what about the family? Wouldn’t they be hurt? Isn’t it selfish?” I calmly replied, “It probably is, but you as a parent would never want anything bad for your kid. I have a kid; she’s 15, and there is nothing I wouldn’t do for her. You wouldn’t feel selfish when you have a kid; you will want to endure for their happiness.” But then Sam went on and said something that ruined it all, with a cheerful smile on his face: “I think you are right. I do feel lonely and hugely responsible. He brought me here, gave me everything I have to the best of his abilities, and he is happy about it. Maybe I am not cursed; maybe I do have a hope. I never asked him this, but he always had peace on his face. I see your point. Thank you for meeting me today.” We had just reached my home, and I would have gone in happily, and nothing would have happened; he shouldn’t have said that. I had to, Susan, so I asked him to come in. I said with a big smile, “I’m glad I could help you, Sam. You seem like a decent guy. And you are pretty smart too. If it’s okay with you, I’d like you to meet my daughter; I feel you two would be good friends. And maybe you could talk to her about what you talked with me; that’s more age-appropriate.” I laughed at my snarky remark, and so did he; he agreed to join for a coffee and came in.

As he came inside, I grabbed him and put some chloroform on his mouth, knocking him unconscious. I then picked him up and took him to the basement, carefully tying him to the chair. I put another chair in front of him and sat there, staring at him, imagining how it would all be different if he hadn’t said that. He was deeply hurt; he understood his situation, yet found hope. I find people like Sam very disturbing; these are the people who have their eyes open but choose to close them. He woke up a little while later, feeling drowsy, slowly opening his eyes, gaining his consciousness back every second. Right when he realized his situation, his squeaking voice went silent, and fear flushed on his face. I could see the terror on his face, but that didn’t worry me too much; that was expected. I calmly leaned forward, looked into his eyes, and said, “Sam, I am going to kill you, but trust me, ‘kill’ is not the emotion that’s correct here; I am going to save you.” “AAaaarrghghhhhh Hee…ll…pppppp…..” he screamed at the top of his lungs, looking straight into my eyes, ready to break any second now. I leaned back into the chair and looked at him; he screamed for 10 minutes straight, or maybe it was 20; I am not sure. I was so alive, I couldn’t focus on trivialities. I waited until he was quiet. I knew he would scream; they all do. After being with so many people, I sincerely believe in the five stages of grief now. The screaming was, of course, denial: not able to understand that they don’t have what they once had, the first realization of loss. But I was intrigued to see the next stage—anger. I have seen two types of anger in people: one where they lash out, threatening, even though they probably realize they can’t do anything. Everything that gave them the confidence to be angry was no more. I don’t like these people because you should be done with that in the denial phase, right? Anyway, I had high expectations from Sam, and he did not disappoint. He calmed down, with little sobs in between his words, and said, “I can’t believe I trusted you; you were just a stranger; I let you in once, and you did this. Don’t you feel bad for doing this to me? What did I do to you?” and he broke down into tears. This was the second type: being angry at themselves, quietly, understanding the situation and curious as to what ended them there. I love that humans always strive for answers, even when they don’t know if they want to hear the answer. I let out a calming smile and said, “Sam, I really respect you; the life you have lived so far has been full of pain, but you didn’t give up; you kept trying because you felt that giving up would hurt people. I wanted to let you go after seeing the anger you had for your father, for you were one who understood the truth and had the strength to go through with it. I really felt good talking to you; I could see myself in you. But understand, I needed to check your will. I needed to make sure that no one could change your mind. And unfortunately, you did. You shouldn’t have, Sam. I am very sorry for you. You lack the strength you accuse others of not having.” As I said this, I could see a pinch of hope on his face; he felt, like everyone else, that since he now knew his fault, he could plead his way out of this. He said, still sobbing but more hopeful this time around, “I am really sorry; I shouldn’t have said that. Maybe I am not that smart, but you know I understand your view, right? You do, right? I promise I’ll change; I’ll be stronger; I won’t let anyone else go through this again. Please leave me; I promise I won’t tell anyone about you. I will change; I promise; I fucking promise!” raising his voice at the end, as if to convince me of his resolve. I let out a laugh at his naivety, saying, “You see, Sam, you are smart; I know that, and you would, of course, tell people if I leave you, as you should. See, maybe you had a chance now if you just stuck to your character, but you couldn’t. I can’t trust you, Sam; you need to be saved.” As I said this, his face dropped, empty of words, shocked, I guess, or maybe he was just curious what comes next; I couldn’t see him so sad. He had said it’s better to have a known enemy, so I decided to tell him about you. His bargaining had ended now, and what he was facing was the depression of the situation. I wanted to help him get to the next stage, so I said, “Sam, I had a daughter; her name was Susan. She was my world, my everything. I still remember the day she was born; I was so happy until I got the news that I had lost Joanna, my wife. I couldn’t look at Susan for weeks; my friends helped me out, but then I realized that what happened to her was far worse, so I let go, Sam; I let go. I started being a good dad. I loved my baby. When she was 10, she was diagnosed with a very rare disease: an early-onset ALS. She would slowly lose control of her body, every part failing one after another, until she was trapped in her mind, unable to speak, unable to cry. I went through everything you are going through, Sam; I was you, but even worse, I was your father. I had brought Susan into this world, into this cursed world which can’t bear the happiness of people. Everyone here is sad. But anyway, as her disease progressed over the years, I left my job, started taking care of her, but things seemed bleaker every day. The doctors had declared her terminal, and she was smart, Sam; she was as smart as you. She had already accepted her life and wanted to go. It took me time to understand her decision, even accept it. But I couldn’t see my baby in pain, so I agreed; I filed a case for doctor-assisted euthanasia. I had thought that was the hardest thing to do.” I let out a big laugh, as if I was laughing at the past me. I love you, Susan, and when I remember it, it still hurts. “Wha…ttt ha..ppe…nned to her?” Sam forced his words. I said, “What do you think, Sam? The court rejected the plea. Apparently, they decided for her that her life still was worth living and there was a hope for a new cure every other day. I was struck when I heard them say this. I went home and told her, with my face resting on her face as I couldn’t stop crying, but she put her hand on my shoulder and comforted me, saying that it was going to be okay; she’ll be out soon anyway. But we both knew that was not true; what was going to follow shouldn’t be experienced by anyone. I put her to bed and went outside, but as soon as I closed the door, I could hear a faint cry from her room. I couldn’t control myself and fell on my knees; my baby was crying, and there was nothing that I could do. I stayed there for hours, staring blankly at the ceiling, until I remembered my vow of doing anything for her. I quietly went into her room and woke her up and told her I was going to save her. She burst out in tears and hugged me. We stayed there for some time, maybe 10 minutes or 20; I can’t focus on trivialities here, Sam. Then I put her on some anesthesia and held her head, talking to her until she fell asleep, for the last time. Sam, trust me, it hurt me a lot, but it had to be done; she didn’t deserve any of that, and all I did was what I should have done. So, after her, I decided it was my responsibility to save people like you from the eternal torment of life.” Sam raised his head slightly, looking me in my eyes and declaring with a straight face, “I understand. I do. And honestly, I have been thinking of doing this but couldn’t get the courage up. I should be thankful to you. Can I ask for a favor though?” Tell me, Susan, how can I deprive a person from their final wish, a final desire which I could fulfill? And so I agreed. “Can you please send my mother a voice note?” I agreed to it and took my phone out. He said, “Mom, it’s Sam. I am going, Mom; this man is freeing me. I tried to explain everything to him, but I couldn’t; I am sorry, Mom. Our lives are very difficult, Mom, and I feel so bad for being this incapable of changing it, especially right now. I just want you to know I am going relieved, Mom; I love you.” His face became expressionless, Susan; that’s what interests me the most; that’s why I am writing this to you. He went to acceptance; no one before ever has. He calmly asked, his tears dried now, “How will I go?” I replied with equal determinism: “I care for you, Sam; I will put you to sleep; you’ll not feel any pain. I will then inject you with this,” I said, and pulled out the snake head. You remember the snake head, right? I got it a few weeks before, and some snake venom; I could now puncture someone with that. I devised a way so that it doesn’t look bad. I could see peace on his face now. I got up from my chair and untied him. I asked him to lie on the bed. Ahh, I forgot to tell you: I have redecorated the basement; it looks beautiful now. I knew you liked spending time here. There is a bed on one side of the room, fairy lights on the wall around, all yellow, giving the room positive vibes. I have two chairs, properly padded for comfort, and a TV. I stay here all the time; it keeps me grounded, pun intended. He lay down on the bed, and I injected him with the anesthesia. We talked about how what he was doing was not unfair to anyone. He was really interested in the snake head; I told him, “I like to make the death interesting. A person having an interesting life is not really in their hands, but having an interesting death sure is. And this sure is interesting.”

We talked until his last thoughts came up, and as he fell asleep, a tear rolled down his eyes, and seeing that, I sobbed too, but just for a moment. He is gone now. You know I don’t believe in the afterlife; that’s how I like to live my life; it gives me comfort, Susan. But when I die, I would believe that there existed something beyond; I want to die with hope, so I told him, my final words to him: “Don’t worry, Sam; you’ll meet many good people up there. Goodbye!” He is gone now, Susan, and I am going to save his mother next, but I will leave his father unharmed; that man must see what he did and relive it fresh every day. Anyway, Susan, he is lying on the bed; I need to plan what to do with his body; that’s the hardest part, you know. I just know, Susan, that all lives are not worth living, and I don’t want anyone else to endure what you had to, so I will continue to do so until I can. I love you, Susan; I always will, and I hope to see you soon.

Best,
Dad
